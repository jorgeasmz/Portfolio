## Security assumption

Classical key exchange rests on computational hardness. RSA is safe because factoring large
numbers is slow on available hardware, which is a claim about engineering rather than a
theorem, and a sufficiently large quantum computer retires it.

BB84 replaces that assumption with a physical constraint. Its guarantee does not depend on how
much computing power an adversary owns; it depends on the fact that measuring an unknown quantum
state disturbs it. An eavesdropper cannot listen without leaving a trace, and the protocol is
arranged so the legitimate parties can quantify the disturbance.

## Protocol and sifting

Alice generates random bits and, independently, picks a random basis for each one: rectilinear
or diagonal. She encodes each bit in the basis she drew and sends the qubit onward.

Bob measures each arrival in a basis he also picks at random, knowing nothing of Alice's choices.
When he happens to match her basis, he recovers her bit exactly. When he does not, his outcome is
random.

Only afterwards do they compare bases over a public channel and discard every position where
they disagree. This is **sifting**, and roughly half the transmission survives it. Announcing the
bases costs nothing because the basis is not the secret: the bit value is, and it is never
revealed.

## Simulated scenarios

The simulator runs the protocol under three conditions:

**Ideal channel.** Alice and Bob's sifted keys agree completely. The error rate is zero, which is
the reference every other run is read against.

**Noisy channel.** Physical imperfection flips some bits. The keys diverge slightly even though
nobody is listening.

**Eavesdropper present.** Eve intercepts each qubit, measures it and forwards a replacement. She
faces the same problem Bob does: she must guess a basis. When she guesses wrong, which happens
half the time, her measurement collapses the state into the wrong basis and the qubit she
forwards carries no relation to Alice's bit. Bob then reads it correctly only half the time.

That gives intercept-resend a specific signature. Half of Eve's guesses are wrong, and half of
those produce a wrong bit at Bob: an expected **25% error rate on the sifted key**, where an
undisturbed channel would show none. The simulator computes exactly this **QBER** and surfaces it
as the detection signal.

## Ambiguity of the error rate

Noise and eavesdropping both raise the QBER, and the protocol cannot tell them apart.

No measurement available to Alice and Bob separates an error caused by fibre imperfection from
one caused by interception, so the conservative reading is the only safe one: **every error is
attributed to an adversary**. Real deployments set a threshold, abort above it and discard the
key. Running the noisy scenario alongside the eavesdropper scenario exhibits that ambiguity,
which any practical QKD system has to be engineered around.

## Interface

The interface walks the protocol stage by stage rather than reporting a final number: Alice's
bits and bases, the prepared qubits, Bob's basis choices and measurements, and the classical
post-processing that produces the sifted key. Alongside it are the statistics that make the run
legible, including histograms of measured bits, the error rate and the degree of key agreement,
plus an explanatory section covering the encoding and decoding of bits in quantum states.

Qiskit performs state preparation and measurement, so the reported statistics arise from the
simulated quantum states rather than from sampling the expected distribution directly. Streamlit
keeps the exploration in the browser.

## Research context

This simulator sits alongside separate academic work on quantum key distribution at CIBioFI,
Universidad del Valle: a study of vacuum and weak decoy states as a countermeasure against
photon-number-splitting attacks, combining Qiskit simulations with a physical optical experiment.
That work is documented in the thesis and received a Special Mention for Outstanding Oral
Presentation at the 2nd International Nano & Quantum Workshop (QTECNOS 2024). This repository is
the interactive, teaching-oriented counterpart, and implements the standard protocol.
