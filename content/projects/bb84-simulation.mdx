## Why Key Distribution Became a Physics Problem

Classical key exchange rests on computational hardness. RSA is safe because factoring large
numbers is slow on the machines we have, which is a statement about engineering rather than a
theorem. A sufficiently large quantum computer removes that assumption.

BB84 replaces it with a physical law. Its security does not depend on how much computing power
an adversary owns; it depends on the fact that measuring an unknown quantum state disturbs it.
An eavesdropper cannot listen without leaving evidence, and the protocol is built so the
legitimate parties can measure exactly how much evidence there is.

## How the Protocol Works

Alice encodes each bit in one of two **conjugate bases**, chosen at random: rectilinear or
diagonal. Bob measures each incoming state, also picking his basis at random and independently.

Neither knows the other's choices while the transmission happens. Afterwards they compare bases
over a public channel and discard every position where they disagree, keeping roughly half the
bits. This step is called **sifting**, and it is why the basis choices can be announced without
weakening anything: the bit values are never revealed, only which basis was used.

The security comes from what happens when someone intercepts. An eavesdropper must also guess a
basis, and half the time she guesses wrong. Measuring in the wrong basis collapses the state and
randomises what Bob receives. The result is a measurable error rate on positions where Alice and
Bob *did* agree, the **QBER**. Above a threshold, they abort. The channel does not need to be
trusted; it needs to be characterised.

## The Problem With Real Hardware

Textbook BB84 assumes a source that emits exactly one photon per pulse. Real sources are
attenuated lasers, and their output follows a Poisson distribution: most pulses are empty, some
carry one photon, and some carry more than one.

Multi-photon pulses are the vulnerability. An eavesdropper can split one photon off, keep it,
and forward the rest untouched, learning part of the key without raising the error rate at all.
This is the **photon-number-splitting attack**, and against it the plain protocol has no defence.

**Decoy states** close the gap. Alice varies the intensity of her pulses at random between a
signal level and one or more weaker decoy levels. An eavesdropper cannot tell which is which, so
any attack that selectively favours multi-photon pulses distorts the yields of the intensity
classes differently. Comparing detection statistics across intensities exposes the attack even
though no individual pulse reveals anything.

## What the Simulator Shows

The application runs the protocol end to end and exposes the parts that are normally invisible:
basis choices, sifting, the resulting QBER, and how the yields of signal and decoy pulses
diverge when an eavesdropper is introduced. Parameters are adjustable, so the effect of channel
loss, intensity settings and interception strategy can be explored directly rather than read
about.

Qiskit handles state preparation and measurement; Streamlit provides the interface, so the
underlying quantum mechanics stays in Python and the exploration stays in the browser.

## Research Context

This simulator supports the thesis *Use of Decoy States as a Method for Increasing Security in a
Quantum Key Distribution Protocol*, which paired these simulations with a physical optical
experiment at CIBioFI, Universidad del Valle. The work received a Special Mention for
Outstanding Oral Presentation at the 2nd International Nano & Quantum Workshop (QTECNOS 2024).
